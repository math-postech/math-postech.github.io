Lecture 2: Divisors, Line Bundles, and Picard Groups ​

Student notes: pp. 5–14
Prep source: lecture2.md
Theme: Divisors and line bundles are two equivalent languages for controlling functions on curves.

Overview ​

Lecture 2 moves from general functorial geometry to curves. The main objects are:

  1. smooth projective curves;
  2. divisors and rational equivalence;
  3. line bundles as locally free rank-one sheaves;
  4. line bundles on P1 via transition functions;
  5. the Picard group as divisor classes or line-bundle classes.

1. Curves ​

Following Silverman, a curve C over a field k is a k-variety of dimension 1.

For a smooth curve, each local ring OC,P at a closed point P is a discrete valuation ring. Therefore a rational function has a well-defined order of vanishing or pole at each point.

In this course, the main objects are projective curves. A projective curve is a closed subscheme of Pn cut out by homogeneous equations and having dimension 1.

2. Divisors ​

The divisor group is the free abelian group generated by closed points:

Div(C)={∑P∈CnP(P):nP∈Z, nP=0 for all but finitely many P}.

The degree is

deg⁡(∑nP(P))=∑nP.

The subgroup of degree-zero divisors is denoted

Div0(C).

If f∈k(C)×, define its divisor by

div(f)=∑P∈CordP(f)(P).

This is called a principal divisor, and it always has degree zero on a smooth projective curve.

3. Rational Equivalence ​

Two divisors D1,D2 are rationally equivalent if

D1−D2=div(f)

for some f∈k(C)×.

The divisor class group is

Pic(C)≅Div(C)/{principal divisors}.

The degree-zero part is

Pic0(C)≅Div0(C)/{principal divisors}.

The student notes stress notation: depending on context, one may write Div(C), Divk(C), or Divk¯(C), depending on whether divisors are rational over k or over an algebraic closure.

4. Line Bundles ​

A line bundle on a smooth projective curve C is an invertible sheaf: an OC-module L such that locally

L|U≅OU.

The lecture emphasizes that line bundles are local objects glued by transition functions.

If Ui is an open cover and L|Ui≅OUi, then on overlaps Ui∩Uj the gluing is multiplication by an invertible function

gij∈OC×(Ui∩Uj).

5. Line Bundles on P1 ​

Cover

P1=U0∪U∞

with

U0=Speck[t],U∞=Speck[t−1],

and intersection

U0∩U∞=Speck[t,t−1].

A line bundle is obtained by gluing trivial line bundles on the two opens. The transition function must be a unit in

k[t,t−1]×.

Every such unit is of the form

ctn,c∈k×,n∈Z.

The scalar c can be removed by changing trivializations, so isomorphism classes are classified by n∈Z.

Thus

Pic(P1)≅Z,

with standard generator O(1).

6. From Divisors to Line Bundles ​

Given a divisor

D=∑nP(P),

choose, locally around each point P, a rational function fP with

div(fP)|UP=nP(P).

Then glue the trivial sheaves on UP by the ratios

fQfP

on overlaps. This constructs a line bundle OC(D).

Equivalently, its rational sections are described by

L(D)={f∈k(C):div(f)+D≥0}∪{0}.

7. From Line Bundles Back to Divisors ​

A line bundle together with a rational section s determines a divisor:

div(s)=zeros of s−poles of s.

Changing the rational section changes the divisor by a principal divisor. Therefore the isomorphism class of the line bundle corresponds to a divisor class.

This gives the central equivalence:

{divisors modulo rational equivalence}≅{line bundles up to isomorphism}.

8. Picard Functor Viewpoint ​

The student notes also introduce the functorial version:

PicC(S)={line bundles on C×kS}/≅.

The degree-zero part PicC0 is representable in the cases needed later. For an elliptic curve E, this object is again an elliptic curve.

This is the first appearance of the principle that elliptic curves are self-dual through their Picard varieties.
