## Overview

Lecture 2 moves from general functorial geometry to curves. The main objects are:

1. smooth projective curves;
2. divisors and rational equivalence;
3. line bundles as locally free rank-one sheaves;
4. line bundles on $\mathbb P^1$ via transition functions;
5. the Picard group as divisor classes or line-bundle classes.

---

## 1. Curves

Following Silverman, a curve $C$ over a field $k$ is a $k$-variety of dimension $1$.

For a smooth curve, each local ring $\mathcal O_{C,P}$ at a closed point $P$ is a discrete valuation ring. Therefore a rational function has a well-defined order of vanishing or pole at each point.

In this course, the main objects are projective curves. A projective curve is a closed subscheme of $\mathbb P^n$ cut out by homogeneous equations and having dimension $1$.

---

## 2. Divisors

The divisor group is the free abelian group generated by closed points:

$$
\operatorname{Div}(C)=\left\{\sum_{P\in C} n_P(P): n_P\in\mathbb Z,\ n_P=0\text{ for all but finitely many }P\right\}.
$$

The degree is

$$
\deg\left(\sum n_P(P)\right)=\sum n_P.
$$

The subgroup of degree-zero divisors is denoted

$$
\operatorname{Div}^0(C).
$$

If $f\in k(C)^\times$, define its divisor by

$$
\operatorname{div}(f)=\sum_{P\in C}\operatorname{ord}_P(f)(P).
$$

This is called a principal divisor, and it always has degree zero on a smooth projective curve.

---

## 3. Rational Equivalence

Two divisors $D_1,D_2$ are rationally equivalent if

$$
D_1-D_2=\operatorname{div}(f)
$$

for some $f\in k(C)^\times$.

The divisor class group is

$$
\operatorname{Pic}(C)
\cong
\operatorname{Div}(C)/\{\text{principal divisors}\}.
$$

The degree-zero part is

$$
\operatorname{Pic}^0(C)
\cong
\operatorname{Div}^0(C)/\{\text{principal divisors}\}.
$$

The student notes stress notation: depending on context, one may write $\operatorname{Div}(C)$, $\operatorname{Div}_k(C)$, or $\operatorname{Div}_{\bar k}(C)$, depending on whether divisors are rational over $k$ or over an algebraic closure.

---

## 4. Line Bundles

A line bundle on a smooth projective curve $C$ is an invertible sheaf: an $\mathcal O_C$-module $\mathcal L$ such that locally

$$
\mathcal L|_U\cong \mathcal O_U.
$$

The lecture emphasizes that line bundles are local objects glued by transition functions.

If $U_i$ is an open cover and $\mathcal L|_{U_i}\cong\mathcal O_{U_i}$, then on overlaps $U_i\cap U_j$ the gluing is multiplication by an invertible function

$$
g_{ij}\in\mathcal O_C^\times(U_i\cap U_j).
$$

---

## 5. Line Bundles on $\mathbb P^1$

Cover

$$
\mathbb P^1=U_0\cup U_\infty
$$

with

$$
U_0=\operatorname{Spec}k[t],\qquad
U_\infty=\operatorname{Spec}k[t^{-1}],
$$

and intersection

$$
U_0\cap U_\infty=\operatorname{Spec}k[t,t^{-1}].
$$

A line bundle is obtained by gluing trivial line bundles on the two opens. The transition function must be a unit in

$$
k[t,t^{-1}]^\times.
$$

Every such unit is of the form

$$
c t^n,
\qquad c\in k^\times,
\quad n\in\mathbb Z.
$$

The scalar $c$ can be removed by changing trivializations, so isomorphism classes are classified by $n\in\mathbb Z$.

Thus

$$
\operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb P^1)\cong\mathbb Z,
$$

with standard generator $\mathcal O(1)$.

---

## 6. From Divisors to Line Bundles

Given a divisor

$$
D=\sum n_P(P),
$$

choose, locally around each point $P$, a rational function $f_P$ with

$$
\operatorname{div}(f_P)|_{U_P}=n_P(P).
$$

Then glue the trivial sheaves on $U_P$ by the ratios

$$
\frac{f_Q}{f_P}
$$

on overlaps. This constructs a line bundle $\mathcal O_C(D)$.

Equivalently, its rational sections are described by

$$
L(D)=\{f\in k(C):\operatorname{div}(f)+D\ge 0\}\cup\{0\}.
$$

---

## 7. From Line Bundles Back to Divisors

A line bundle together with a rational section $s$ determines a divisor:

$$
\operatorname{div}(s)=\text{zeros of }s-\text{poles of }s.
$$

Changing the rational section changes the divisor by a principal divisor. Therefore the isomorphism class of the line bundle corresponds to a divisor class.

This gives the central equivalence:

$$
\left\{\text{divisors modulo rational equivalence}\right\}
\cong
\left\{\text{line bundles up to isomorphism}\right\}.
$$

---

## 8. Picard Functor Viewpoint

The student notes also introduce the functorial version:

$$
\operatorname{Pic}_C(S)=
\left\{\text{line bundles on }C\times_k S\right\}/\cong.
$$

The degree-zero part $\operatorname{Pic}^0_C$ is representable in the cases needed later. For an elliptic curve $E$, this object is again an elliptic curve.

This is the first appearance of the principle that elliptic curves are self-dual through their Picard varieties.
